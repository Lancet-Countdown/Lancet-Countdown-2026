# Methods

This indicator takes data from the **International Health Regulations (IHR (2005)) State Party Self-Assessment Annual Reporting Tool (SPAR)**.

Under the IHR (2005), all States Parties are required to have or to develop minimum core public health capacities to implement the IHR (2005) effectively. IHR (2005) also states that all States Parties should report annually to the World Health Assembly on the implementation of IHR (2005).

To facilitate this process, WHO developed an IHR Monitoring Questionnaire, interpreting the Core Capacity Requirements in Annex 1 of IHR (2005) into 35 indicators for 15 capacities.

Since 2010, this self-reporting IHR monitoring questionnaire has been sent annually to National IHR Focal Points (NFPs) for data collection. It contains a checklist of 35 indicators specifically developed for monitoring the development and implementation of 15 IHR capacities.

The method of estimation calculates the proportion or percentage of attributes—a set of specific elements or functions which reflect the level of performance or achievement of a specific indicator—reported to be in place in a country.

The core capacities to implement the IHR (2005) have been established by a technical group of experts as those capacities required to:

- Detect events
- Assess events
- Notify and report events
- Respond to public health risks and emergencies of national and international concern

To assess the development and strengthening of core capacities, a set of components is measured for each core capacity by considering one to three indicators that measure the status and progress in developing and strengthening the IHR core capacities.

Each indicator is assessed using a group of specific elements referred to as **attributes**, which represent a complex set of activities or elements required to carry out each component.

At the beginning of 2018, in compliance with the recommendations of the IHR Review Committee on Second Extensions for Establishing National Public Health Capacities and on IHR Implementation, and following formal global consultations with States Parties held in 2015, 2016, 2017, and 2018, the WHO Secretariat replaced the IHR Monitoring Questionnaire with the **IHR State Party Self-Assessment Annual Reporting (SPAR) Tool**.

The SPAR tool scoring system has remained the same since 2018 and is summarised in Table 36.

## Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted for each year, computing:

- Unweighted means
- Medians
- Population-weighted averages

Population-weighted averages used **UN World Population Prospects** estimates and were stratified by:

- WHO region
- HDI group
- Lancet Countdown region

The proportion of countries achieving **high or very high implementation of Capacity 7 (score ≥61)** was calculated alongside the share of the global population covered by such systems.

Trend analysis examined the net change in Capacity 7 scores between **2022 and 2025** at the country level.

Countries were classified into trajectory categories based on the direction of year-on-year changes:

- Sustained improvement
- Sustained deterioration
- Stagnation
- Fluctuation

The association between **Capacity 7** and **Capacity 3.2** was assessed using **Spearman's rank correlation**, globally and stratified by HDI group.

This was complemented by a quadrant analysis relative to global median scores.

## Caveats and Limitations

There are some limitations to considering these capacities as proxies of health system adaptive capacity and system resilience.

First, responses to the IHR monitoring questionnaire are **self-reported**.

Second, the countries reporting IHR implementation differ from year to year within regional aggregate scores.

Third, IHR Core Capacity Requirements are not specific to climate change. Therefore, while they provide a proxy baseline, they do not directly measure a country's adaptive capacity in relation to changes in climate-driven risk.

Fourth, these findings capture **potential capacity rather than action**.

Finally, the quality of surveillance for early detection and warning is not shown, nor is the impact of that surveillance on public health.

Response systems have been inadequate in numerous public health emergencies, and therefore the presence of such plans is not a proxy for their effectiveness.

Nevertheless, these capacities provide a useful starting point for considering the potential adaptive capacity of health systems globally.
