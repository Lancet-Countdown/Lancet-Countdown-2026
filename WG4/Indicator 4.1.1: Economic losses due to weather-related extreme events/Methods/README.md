# Methods

This indicator uses data from the **EM-DAT Emergency Events Database**, provided by UCLouvain / CRED (Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters).

The database contains information on more than **27,000 disasters worldwide since 1900**. For this indicator, data before **2000** are excluded because earlier records are particularly affected by reporting biases.

EM-DAT compiles information from a range of sources, including:

- United Nations agencies
- non-governmental organisations
- insurance companies
- research institutes
- press agencies

An event is included in EM-DAT if it meets at least one of the following criteria:

- 10 or more fatalities
- 100 or more people affected
- declaration of a state of emergency
- call for international assistance

## Weather-related disasters included

EM-DAT includes both natural and technological disasters.

Technological disasters, including industrial accidents, transport disasters and other accidents such as explosions, are excluded from this indicator because they are not weather-related.

Among natural disasters, biological hazards such as epidemics and infestations, and geophysical hazards such as earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanic activity, are also excluded.

The weather-related hazards included in this indicator are:

| Hazard group | Subtypes |
|---|---|
| Climatological | Drought; Wildfire, including forest fire, bush fire, brush fire, pasture fire and general wildfire; Glacial lake outburst flood |
| Hydrological | Flood; Wet mass movements, including landslides, mudslides and avalanches |
| Meteorological | Storms, including cyclones, tornadoes, thunderstorms, hail, blizzards and sandstorms; Temperature-related events, including heat waves, cold waves and wintry weather |

## Data coverage

The EM-DAT database is continuously updated.

The version used for this indicator was produced on **20 March 2026**. Data are validated at the beginning of the following year, making **2025** the most recent year with validated data.

## Economic losses

Economic losses were reported in both nominal values and **2024 US dollars**, except for 2025, where only nominal values were available.

All economic losses were converted to **2025 US dollars** using Consumer Price Index data from the International Monetary Fund.

Losses are reported:

- globally
- by Human Development Index group
- by WHO region
- by Lancet Countdown region

Economic losses are presented both as:

- absolute losses
- losses relative to GDP

GDP values are primarily obtained from the **International Monetary Fund**, with missing country values supplemented using **World Bank** data.

Nominal GDP values are converted to **2025 US dollars** using IMF CPI data.

# Data

The indicator uses the following data sources:

- **Weather-related disasters:** EM-DAT Emergency Events Database, UCLouvain / CRED
- **Consumer Price Index and GDP:** IMF World Economic Outlook database
- **Supplementary country GDP:** World Bank World Development Indicators

# Caveats and Limitations

The EM-DAT database has several known limitations.

The quality and completeness of the data depend on the availability of reporting sources and on how effectively disasters are documented in different countries.

Some events may be missing or have incomplete information, and reporting practices vary across countries.

Certain hazards, particularly **heatwaves**, are more likely to be underreported than others.

High-impact disasters generally receive greater media attention and are more likely to be recorded, while events close to the EM-DAT reporting thresholds may be more likely to be missed.

Economic losses are reported less consistently than human impact measures such as mortality.

Insured losses are generally reported more frequently than uninsured losses, creating geographic bias in regions with lower insurance coverage. For this reason, insured losses are not reported in this indicator.

EM-DAT data coverage has improved substantially over the past 30 to 40 years as source reporting has improved, although gaps and data quality issues remain.

Despite these limitations, EM-DAT estimates are broadly similar in magnitude to those reported in commercial disaster datasets and provide a useful basis for tracking the scale and long-term trends of economic losses from extreme weather events.
