# Methods

Our approach is based on a bottom-up aggregation of published grant-level data. Major funders of research publish information on funded projects, typically providing a project description or abstract. This data enables identification of health R&D on specific themes and issues, based on the text of the project description.

## Dimensions Database

Data is sourced from the Dimensions database:

https://www.dimensions.ai/dimensions-data/

The Dimensions database has translated and aggregated information on project grants. Their database reports more than 8 million grants from just over 700 funders globally, from 44 countries.

The database includes detailed project-level information, including:

- Title
- Abstract
- Information about the funded organisations
- Information about the funders

## Defining Climate and Health Research

Our indicator aims to measure the way in which health funders are responding to climate driven changes in the burden of disease.

We are therefore interested in research grants on health outcomes associated with climate change and hazard exposures created or exacerbated by climate change.

We exclude grants focused on reducing greenhouse gas emissions (GHG), since our focus is on grants that addresses climate change impacts on human health.

We also exclude health co-benefits of GHG reduction policies, such as the air pollution reductions associated with electrification of transport.

## Caveats and Limitations

There are clear weaknesses in the underlying grant-level data.

First, not all research funders provide transparent information about funded projects, particularly funders in lower-income countries. The data are dominated by countries that have greater transparency in their research funding systems. These countries tend both to be richer countries and those that fund the great majority of health research, on both a per-GDP basis and in absolute terms.

For regional and country analysis, this means that there are substantial gaps. Only two African countries are found in the data, and even for these countries the data on funding amounts, rather than grant numbers, are extremely limited.

Data are also weak for South America, with data only available for Brazil, Chile, and Uruguay, and only for grant numbers rather than values.

Data for Asia are dominated by East Asia (Japan, South Korea, China and Taiwan), with weak or absent data for many other Asian countries, particularly those with low incomes.

A notable weakness in the data is limited coverage of research funding in China. Grants from China’s National Natural Science Foundation are included in the data, but only up to 2020.

The data reflect the choices made by the largest funders of health research globally and, despite coverage weaknesses, can therefore be considered broadly representative of global health research trends.

WHO data on total health funding in 74 countries suggest that high-income countries spend vastly more on health research than middle- and low-income countries. High-income countries spend around 0.27% of GDP on health research, much higher than upper-middle-income countries (0.03%) or lower-middle-income and low-income countries (less than 0.02%).

Combined with the effects of higher GDP in high-income countries, these figures demonstrate the extent to which research trends among funders in higher-income countries provide a good description of global trends in health research.

However, regional detail—and therefore differences in regional patterns of research funding allocation—is only available for:

- North America
- East Asia
- Europe
- Oceania
- A small number of countries elsewhere, including Brazil, Chile, Russia until 2022, South Africa and Israel

An important limitation of the data is that it is not possible to identify the research priorities of low-income countries, as expressed through their allocation of research grants.

A further weakness is that the data only represent project grants. In most countries, research funding is distributed both through project grants and through core funding to research institutions such as universities or national laboratories.

The allocation of such **core** or **block** funding to specific research themes is not visible from grant-level data.

We can provide an approximate estimate of the coverage of health-related R&D in the Dimensions database by comparing it with other estimates of total global health research.

The only figure we can identify that enables a direct comparison, i.e. focused on global publicly funded (governments and foundations) health-related R&D spending, is from Chakma et al. (2014), for which the most recent year provided is 2012.

Dimensions data report global health R&D from funders (governments and foundations) at around **USD $56 billion in 2012**, while Chakma et al. estimated around **USD $102 billion**.

This suggests that Dimensions data cover a large share of global health-related R&D, but it also highlights the limitations of the data.

In particular, the omission of core (not grant-specific) funding to research institutions means that the total volumes of funding are underestimated by Dimensions. It is also not known whether core funding might differ from grant funding in terms of the focus of research priorities.

It is important to note that the relative share of research funding delivered through project-specific grants rather than core institutional funds differs by country.

This means that care should be taken when comparing absolute amounts of climate-related health research between countries, since differences in absolute amounts may reflect research funding models as much as real differences in the scale of research funding.

The ratio of climate-related health research to total health research is more straightforward, enabling comparison of the relative focus of grant funding in health research on climate change.

Data on grants in China substantially understate total levels of public research expenditure, both because a large proportion of funding is delivered institutionally rather than through projects and because of limited coverage in Dimensions of the full range of grant-funding organisations in China.

Comparing figures for 2019, China’s National Bureau of Statistics reports total government R&D investments in that year of around **US$78.8 billion**, while the value of all grants starting in that year in Dimensions accounts for only **US$3.9 billion**.

Our approach aims to track public spending on health-related R&D by governments and charitable foundations. This includes funders associated with:

- National governments
- International organisations, such as the WHO and European Commission
- National and international charitable foundations, such as the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation and the Wellcome Trust

It is important to note that we omit private-sector spending, despite this playing a critical role in global health systems. Private businesses are estimated to account for around 60% of health-related R&D spending globally.

However, data on private spending on health R&D by topic are extremely limited, as such data are often commercially sensitive.

We therefore focus on public spending, which is also more directly policy relevant because it relates directly to public policy choices.

There are specific limitations in the data available on grant recipients. Where there are multiple recipients of a grant, funding amounts are not available for each recipient.

In estimating the share of funding received by low-HDI countries, we have assumed that when there are two or more recipient countries on a grant, the grant is split equally between the countries.

This may introduce some bias into the results. However, the share of grants with multiple recipient countries is small, and therefore any resulting bias would not change the overall conclusions.

## Limitations to the Search Strategy

There are also limitations to the search strategy, which is based on keywords and machine-learning-derived research classifications produced by Dimensions. These can result in both false positives and false negatives.

It is important to be aware of the limitations of keyword-based classification when drawing conclusions from comparisons across countries, since cultural differences in the style of project descriptions may influence the apparent climate-relatedness of research.

For example, some countries may expect grant descriptions to provide more discussion of the wider context for the research, including climate change, whereas others may be more narrowly focused on describing the details of the proposed research.
