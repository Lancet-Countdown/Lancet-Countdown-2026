# Methods

## Fossil Fuel Subsidies

Data on fossil fuel subsidies were obtained from two sources: the **International Energy Agency (IEA)** and the **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)**.

The IEA provides estimates of fossil fuel consumption subsidies for **47 countries** using its **price-gap approach**. This calculates the difference between the end-user price paid for fossil fuels in a country and a reference price that reflects the full cost of supply.

The countries covered by the IEA dataset are mainly non-OECD countries.

The OECD provides estimates of fossil fuel subsidies for the **38 OECD countries**, together with:

- Argentina
- Armenia
- Azerbaijan
- Brazil
- China
- Croatia
- Georgia
- India
- Indonesia
- Moldova
- Romania
- South Africa

This normally covers 50 countries. However, the **United States did not report fossil fuel subsidies for the 2026 report**, reducing the OECD coverage to 49 countries.

OECD estimates are based on a bottom-up inventory of individual subsidy mechanisms within each country and include:

- Production support
- Consumption support
- Infrastructure investment
- Incentives
- Research and development

OECD support measures are grouped into three broad categories:

- Consumer Support Estimate (CSE)
- Producer Support Estimate (PSE)
- General Services Support Estimate (GSE)

Combining the IEA and OECD datasets provides coverage of **81 countries**, after accounting for overlaps and excluding countries not covered by the Lancet Countdown.

## Combining IEA and OECD subsidy estimates

Some countries are included in both the IEA and OECD datasets.

The OECD provides an approach for reconciling the two sources by identifying OECD support measures that most closely correspond to the IEA price-gap definition, particularly measures that reduce prices paid by consumers.

However, analysis of countries covered by both datasets showed that this approach did not consistently reconcile the estimates. In several cases, removing OECD measures that appeared less directly related to consumer prices increased the difference between the OECD and IEA estimates.

For this indicator, the **total OECD estimate** and **total IEA estimate** were therefore compared directly for overlapping countries.

Following a simple OECD rule of thumb, the source producing the **larger cumulative subsidy total over the period analysed** was selected for each overlapping country.

## Carbon Prices and Revenues

Carbon pricing and carbon pricing revenue data were obtained from the **World Bank Carbon Pricing Dashboard**.

Revenue from each carbon pricing instrument was allocated to the country in which the instrument operated.

### EU Emissions Trading System

Revenue from the **EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS)** was allocated among participating countries.

This included the 28 EU member states during the period when the United Kingdom was a member, together with Iceland and Norway.

EU ETS revenues were allocated according to each participating country's share of total CO2 emissions among EU ETS participants.

The United Kingdom was allocated a share of EU ETS revenues through the end of **2020**. Although the UK formally left the European Union on 31 January 2020, it remained subject to EU rules until 31 December 2020.

The UK's own Emissions Trading System replaced participation in the EU ETS from **1 January 2021**.

Liechtenstein is also a member of the EU ETS but does not have CO2 emissions reported by the IEA.

Liechtenstein's CO2 emissions were therefore estimated by assuming the same **CO2-to-population ratio as OECD Europe**, combined with United Nations population data.

Carbon pricing revenue was reported for El Salvador in 2010, but no subsequent revenue has been reported.

After allocating EU ETS revenues, **46 countries reported carbon pricing revenue in 2023**, compared with 33 countries in 2010.

## Net Carbon Price and Revenue Calculations

Carbon pricing and fossil fuel subsidies are generally applied to particular fuels or sectors rather than across an entire economy.

The sectors covered by carbon prices and fossil fuel subsidies can also differ between countries.

To provide a consistent measure across countries, both fossil fuel subsidies and carbon pricing revenues were averaged across total economy-wide CO2 emissions.

For each country:

```math
\text{Net Carbon Revenue}
=
\text{Carbon Pricing Revenue}
-
\text{Fossil Fuel Subsidies}
```

Net carbon revenue was then divided by total annual CO2 emissions:

```math
\text{Net Carbon Price}
=
\frac{\text{Net Carbon Revenue}}
{\text{Total CO}_2\text{ Emissions}}
```

The resulting net carbon price is negative where fossil fuel subsidies exceed carbon pricing revenues.

Countries were included where data were available for:

- CO2 emissions, and
- either fossil fuel subsidies or carbon pricing instruments

This resulted in **87 countries being included in 2024**, accounting for approximately **81% of global CO2 emissions**.

The lower share of global emissions compared with the previous report reflects the exclusion of the United States because fossil fuel subsidy data were not reported for the current analysis.

## Net Carbon Revenue Relative to Health Expenditure

Net carbon revenue was also expressed relative to national expenditure on health.

Annual current health expenditure data were obtained from the **WHO Global Health Expenditure Database**.

Capital health expenditure is not included.

The comparison therefore provides an estimate of the scale of net carbon revenues relative to each country's current annual health expenditure.

## Estimating Missing CO2 and Health Expenditure Data

The most recent year with a complete set of source data is **2023**.

To extend the indicator to **2024**, missing CO2 emissions and health expenditure values were estimated using recent trends.

### CO2 emissions

Where 2024 CO2 emissions were unavailable, values were estimated using linear trends in recent years.

The year **2020** was excluded from these trends because CO2 emissions were unusually low during the COVID-19 pandemic.

### Health expenditure

Where 2024 health expenditure data were unavailable, values were similarly estimated using recent trends.

The years **2020 and 2021** were excluded because health expenditure during the COVID-19 pandemic was unusually high in some countries and unusually low in others.

The resulting 2024 net carbon price and net carbon revenue relative to health expenditure therefore contain estimated values for some countries.

## Currency Standardisation

All monetary values are expressed in **real 2025 US dollars**.

IEA and OECD fossil fuel subsidy data were provided in real 2024 US dollars and were converted to real 2025 values using the **US dollar GDP deflator from the IMF**.

World Bank carbon pricing revenue and WHO health expenditure data were originally reported in nominal US dollars.

These values were also converted to real 2025 US dollars using the IMF US dollar GDP deflator.

# Data

The indicator uses the following data sources:

- **Fossil fuel subsidies:** IEA Fossil Fuel Subsidies Database
- **Fossil fuel subsidies:** OECD Inventory of Support Measures for Fossil Fuels
- **Carbon pricing:** World Bank Carbon Pricing Dashboard
- **CO2 emissions from fuel combustion:** IEA Greenhouse Gas Emissions from Energy
- **Health expenditure:** WHO Global Health Expenditure Database
- **US dollar GDP deflator:** IMF World Economic Outlook database

# Caveats and Limitations

The indicator is strongly dependent on the completeness and reliability of the underlying IEA, OECD and World Bank datasets.

Country-level data may be incomplete because of reporting errors, missing information or differences in reporting practices.

The indicator should therefore primarily be interpreted as a measure of **global and regional trends**, and caution should be used when interpreting results for individual countries.

The most recent year with a complete set of source data is **2023**.

CO2 emissions and health expenditure values for some countries in **2024** were estimated from recent trends. These estimates will be replaced or confirmed when complete source data become available.

The economy-wide net carbon price is calculated by dividing fossil fuel subsidies and carbon pricing revenues by total CO2 emissions.

This approach aligns closely with fossil fuel subsidies because fossil fuel use is the principal source of CO2 emissions.

However, some carbon pricing instruments also apply to sectors and greenhouse gases beyond fossil fuel combustion.

There is therefore a small inconsistency between the sectoral coverage of fossil fuel subsidies and the carbon pricing instruments included in the indicator.
