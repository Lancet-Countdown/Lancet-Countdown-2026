# Methods

The data for this indicator are sourced from the **2026 IEA World Energy Investment (WEI)** publication.

## Investment category definitions

This indicator uses the investment categories defined in the IEA World Energy Investment report.

### Fossil fuels

**Fossil fuel investment** includes investment in:

- Coal, oil and gas electricity generation capacity
- Fossil fuel supply without carbon capture, utilisation and storage (CCUS)
- Non-renewable waste

Transitional fossil fuels are not included in this category.

### Clean energy

**Clean energy investment** consists of three main components:

1. Clean energy supply
2. Grids and storage
3. End-use investment

### 1. Clean energy supply

Clean energy supply includes investment in power and fuels, including:

- Solar power
- Wind power
- Other renewable power, primarily hydropower
- Nuclear power
- Clean fuels
- Other clean power
- Transitional fossil fuels
- Fossil fuels with CCUS
- Direct air capture

Clean fuels include:

- Modern bioenergy
- Low-emissions hydrogen-based fuels
- Fossil fuels with CCUS
- Direct air capture

Other clean power includes:

- Fossil-fuelled power with CCUS
- Hydrogen
- Ammonia
- Large-scale heat pumps

### 2. Grids and storage

The transition to a clean energy system requires expansion of electricity networks to transport clean electricity to end users.

Energy storage is also considered an important component of the clean energy transition because of the variable nature of electricity generation from many renewable sources.

This category therefore includes:

- Electricity networks
- Battery storage

Pumped hydro is classified under hydropower rather than storage.

### 3. End-use investment

End-use investment, also referred to as **demand-side investment**, includes spending on technologies that improve energy efficiency or support electrification.

Replacing inefficient technologies with more efficient alternatives is classified as clean energy investment because it reduces energy demand.

Electrification is also treated as an enabler of the clean energy transition, including technologies such as electric vehicles and heat pumps.

End-use investment therefore includes two broad categories:

- Energy efficiency
- Electrification

#### Energy efficiency

Energy efficiency investment is defined as incremental spending on new energy-efficient equipment or the full cost of refurbishments that reduce energy use.

#### Electrification

Electrification, also referred to as **other end use**, includes:

- Replacement of fossil-fuelled technologies with electrified alternatives
- Renewable energy for end use
- Hydrogen
- Industrial CCUS

## End-use categories

The end-use technologies included across buildings, transport and industry are summarised below.

| Category | Buildings | Transport | Industry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Energy efficiency** | Building materials, including building envelopes and retrofits; appliances and cooking; water and space heating and cooling; switching from fuel to electricity for heating, cooling and cooking, including heat pumps | Road vehicles, including cars, buses, trucks, and two- and three-wheelers; domestic shipping; rail transport; maritime and aviation | Industrial energy management systems; fuel efficiency; electrical efficiency; use of best available technology |
| **Electrification** | Electrification through heat pumps; renewables for building use, including bioenergy, geothermal, solar home systems, hydrogen and other renewables | Road electrification, including battery, plug-in hybrid and fuel-cell vehicles and EV chargers; rail, shipping and aviation electrification using electricity, hydrogen and bioenergy | Heat pumps for industrial heat, electric boilers and electrification of energy-intensive processes; renewables including bioenergy, geothermal, solar thermal, hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels, and carbon capture |

## Definition of investment

For most sectors, **investment** refers to ongoing capital expenditure on assets.

For some sectors, such as power generation, investment is distributed across the period from the start of construction of a new plant or upgrade to the year in which it becomes operational.

For other sectors, such as upstream oil and gas and liquefied natural gas projects, investment reflects capital spending incurred as production from a new source increases or to maintain production from an existing asset.

This differs from the definition used by the IEA before 2019, when investment was defined as overnight capital expenditure.

## Classification of clean energy

The definition of clean energy used in this indicator follows the definition used in the IEA World Energy Investment report.

Some components included within the clean energy category may be interpreted differently. For example, nuclear power generates radioactive waste but does not produce carbon emissions during electricity generation and is therefore classified as clean energy in this indicator.

Similarly, technologies such as CCUS and transitional fossil fuels may not always be considered clean energy. However, investment in fossil fuels with CCUS, transitional fossil fuels, non-renewable waste, direct air capture and other clean power represents a relatively small proportion of total global energy investment.

The IEA definition is therefore retained to ensure consistency with the World Energy Investment data.

## Clean energy and fossil fuel investment

Previous versions of this indicator separated energy investment between fuels and the power sector.

This distinction can show, for example, that clean energy supply investment is currently concentrated in clean electricity generation, with less investment directed towards clean fuels.

However, this separation does not fully capture investment in electrification technologies such as electric vehicles and heat pumps, which are included within end-use investment but depend on the electricity system.

For this reason, the indicator focuses on the broader distinction between:

- **Clean energy investment**
- **Fossil fuel investment**

This classification is used regardless of whether investment occurs in the fuel or power sectors.

## Regional analysis

The World Energy Investment 2026 report does not provide country-level investment data but reports investment according to regional groupings.

The report uses two different sets of regional classifications:

- regional groupings used in the main report and online data
- regional groupings used in the accompanying data file

Some differences exist between these classifications, including for Europe, Central and South America, and Asia Pacific.

This indicator uses the regional groupings provided in the **World Energy Investment data file**, as this file contains the underlying investment values for these regions.

It is not possible to reclassify these data according to the standard Lancet Countdown regional groupings. The IEA World Energy Investment regional classifications are therefore retained.

The Lancet Countdown regions for **Africa, Europe and Northern America** correspond relatively closely with the equivalent WEI regions, although some differences remain. For example, Mexico is included within North America in the WEI classification.

The correspondence is weaker for some other regions. For example, a substantial proportion of countries classified within the WEI Central and South America region are included in the Lancet Countdown SIDS region.

For this indicator, investment data for **China** are also separated from the Asia Pacific regional total to allow China's investment patterns to be assessed independently from the rest of the Asia Pacific region.

# Data

The indicator uses:

- **IEA World Energy Investment 2026**

# Caveats and Limitations

Some types of expenditure are not included in this indicator, including:

- Operation and maintenance costs
- Research and development
- Financing costs
- Mergers and acquisitions
- Public market transactions

Investment estimates are derived from IEA data on energy demand, supply and trade, together with estimates of unit capacity costs.
